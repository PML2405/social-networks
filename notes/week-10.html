Week 10 — Epidemics: How Diseases Spread on Networks

Lectures 128–142 · from the simple branching model and the basic reproductive number R₀, through the SIR/SIS models and percolation, to a full proof of why R₀ = 1 is the knife-edge between "dies out" and "epidemic".
🧠 the big picture, first

A contagious disease spreads along a network just like an idea — but with two twists: no choice (you can reject a song, not a virus) and invisibility (you rarely know who infected you). To model it you need two things: the pathogen (how contagious, a probability $p$) and the network (dense for flu, sparse for HIV). On a simple tree where each node has $k$ children, the expected number of new infections per case is the basic reproductive number $R_0 = p\cdot k$. The single most important fact of the week: if $R_0 < 1$ the disease dies out for certain; if $R_0 > 1$ it can become an epidemic. We refine the picture with the SIR (measles) and SIS (common cold) models, recast the whole random process statically as percolation (open/closed pipes), and finish with a clean cobweb proof that the slope of one curve at the origin is exactly $R_0$.

LECTURES 128–129

Disease vs idea; the two ingredients

why disease-spread needs its own model, and what that model needs

1 Contagion — but a real one

Week 7 studied social contagion — ideas, behaviours and actions spreading person-to-person (sports shoes, smoking, obesity — if your friend is obese your chance rises ~45%, a friend-of-friend ~25%, three hops ~12%; even depression, happiness and a product like the iPad spread this way). This week drops the word "social" and models actual contagion: a contagious disease like flu. Why bother? To fight epidemics — a contagion that infects a huge fraction of people (Ebola, swine flu, the Black Death). And yes, a network scientist has plenty to say, because a disease spreads on the contact network of the world.

But disease-spread is not identical to idea-spread — there are two crucial differences:

📖 disease-spread ≠ idea-spread
  • No choice. You can hear a song and decide not to adopt it. You cannot decide to refuse a flu virus your friend sneezed onto you — if it infects, it infects.
  • Invisibility. With an idea you often know exactly who you got it from. With a disease you usually can't tell (the professor's story: three family members had eye-flu; he caught it but never knew from whom). So disease-spread is much more of a random process.

The two ingredients of any epidemic model

🦠 the pathogen

How contagious is it? Modelled as a probability $p$ of transmitting across a contact. Measles/flu are highly contagious; Ebola/HIV much less. (Analogy: a juicy gossip spreads faster than a dull piece of code.)

🕸️ the network

Denser networks spread faster. And the network depends on the pathogen: flu spreads on a dense contact network (even a shared keyboard counts); HIV, spreading by sexual contact, lives on a much sparser network.

  1. Name the two differences between how a disease spreads and how an idea spreads.
    No choice (you can't opt out of a virus the way you can ignore an idea) and invisibility (you usually can't identify who infected you). Both make disease-spread a more random process.
  2. Why is the contact network for HIV sparser than the one for flu?
    Because the two pathogens use different transmission routes. Flu passes with casual proximity (many edges → dense); HIV passes via sexual contact (few edges → sparse). The network you draw depends on the pathogen.
LECTURES 130–131

The branching model & expected infections

a tree, a coin per edge, and the expected number of new cases

2 One case, k children, probability p

Start with the simplest possible network: a tree where every node has $k$ children, and every edge transmits the infection with probability $p$. (Real networks have triads and aren't trees — that comes later; for now, simplicity.) If a node is infected, model each child independently by a coin toss: heads (prob $p$) → that child is infected, tails → not.

one infected node, k = 4 children

toss a coin per edge; a head infects that child. Here 3 of 4 were infected this round.

How many children get infected on average? Set up an indicator random variable $X_i$ for each child: $X_i = 1$ if infected (head), $0$ otherwise. Then the total infected is $X = X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_k$.

By linearity of expectation, $E[X] = \sum_i E[X_i]$, and the expectation of an indicator is just the probability it's 1, namely $p$. So $E[X] = \underbrace{p + p + \dots + p}_{k} = p\cdot k$.

expected new infections from one case
$$ E[X] = p_1 + p_2 + \cdots + p_k = p\cdot k $$
read it as: "on average, one infected node passes it to $p\cdot k$ of its $k$ children." Intuition check: toss a fair coin ($p=0.5$) 4 times → expect $0.5\times 4 = 2$ heads. With $p=0.25,\,k=4$ → expect $1$.
💡 spreading down the levels

Level $i$ of the tree holds $k^{\,i}$ people, and the expected number infected at level $i$ is $(p\cdot k)^{\,i}$. So the disease is an epidemic if it survives all the way down to the "infinite" level; otherwise it fizzles out at some finite depth.

  1. A node has $k=6$ children and each edge infects with $p=0.5$. Expected number of children infected?
    $p\cdot k = 0.5\times 6 = \mathbf{3}$. (Sum of 6 indicator variables, each with expectation 0.5.)
  2. Why does linearity of expectation let us ignore whether the coin tosses are independent?
    $E[X_1+\dots+X_k]=E[X_1]+\dots+E[X_k]$ holds regardless of dependence, so we can add the per-edge probabilities directly to get $p\cdot k$.
LECTURE 132

The basic reproductive number R₀

one number that decides epidemic or not

3 R₀ = p·k, and the knife-edge at 1

That number $p\cdot k$ — the expected count of secondary infections produced by one primary case — is the basic reproductive number, written $R_0$. It alone decides the fate of the disease.

basic reproductive number
$$ R_0 = p\cdot k = \text{expected secondary infections per case} $$
casewhat happenscertainty
$R_0 < 1$each case makes fewer than one new case → infection dies outwith probability 1 (certain)
$R_0 > 1$each case makes more than one → infection persists / epidemicwith positive probability (not certain — it could still die if, by chance, every early edge fails)
✍️ worked examples (lecture 132)
  1. Dies out. $p=0.25$, $k=2$ → $R_0 = 0.5 < 1$. Each case infects half a child on average — can't even sustain one — so it vanishes for sure.
  2. Epidemic. $p=0.25$, $k=40$ → $R_0 = 10 > 1$. One case → ~10 → ~100 → ~1000 → … it explodes. $$ \boxed{R_0 = p\cdot k \;\; \begin{cases} <1 \Rightarrow \text{dies out}\\ >1 \Rightarrow \text{epidemic}\end{cases}} $$
🔪 the "knife-edge" property — and how to fight epidemics

Because the threshold sits exactly at 1, nudging $R_0$ across it flips the outcome. A disease with $R_0 = 1.2$ (epidemic) can be killed by pushing it down to $0.9$. Since $R_0 = p\cdot k$, you either lower $p$ (better hygiene, vaccination, public-health ads — reduce the chance of transmission) or lower $k$ (quarantine — isolate the person so they contact fewer people). Either shove drops $R_0$ below 1 and the epidemic collapses.

  1. A disease has $p=0.2$ and each person contacts $k=4$ others. Is it an epidemic, and what could a health authority do?
    $R_0 = 0.2\times 4 = 0.8 < 1$ → it dies out on its own. If instead $R_0$ were above 1, they'd lower $p$ (hygiene/vaccines) or lower $k$ (quarantine) to push $R_0$ below 1.
  2. When $R_0 > 1$, is an epidemic guaranteed?
    No — it persists with positive probability, not certainty. There's always a small chance that every early transmission edge fails and the outbreak dies. When $R_0 < 1$, by contrast, death is certain (probability 1).
LECTURES 133–135

SIR and SIS models

three states, an infection period, and whether the process ever stops

4 Susceptible → Infected → (Recovered or back to Susceptible)

Real networks aren't trees, and a node shouldn't keep infecting forever. The SIR model gives each node one of three states, and it changes over discrete days:

SIR state machine

Susceptible (blue) → Infected (red, for $T_I$ days) → Recovered/Removed (yellow, out of the game).
📖 the three states + the infection period
  • Susceptible — healthy, can catch it.
  • Infected — sick and contagious for a fixed infection period $T_I$ (e.g. 3 days); each day it tries to infect its susceptible neighbours (coin toss, prob $p$).
  • Recovered / Removed — either gained lifetime immunity (measles) or died and left the network. Either way it's out — it can't infect and can't be re-infected.

A node is in exactly one state at a time. "SIR" fits terminal illness (I→removed) or a once-in-a-lifetime disease like measles (I→immune).

⚙️ how one SIR simulation runs (day by day)
  1. Day 0: one node is infected; everyone else susceptible.
  2. Each infected node, for each susceptible neighbour, tosses a biased coin (head prob $p$) — a head infects that neighbour.
  3. After its $T_I$ days are up, an infected node becomes recovered and stops participating.
  4. Repeat. The process stops the moment no node is infected (no red nodes left).

Because coins are tossed, every run can differ — unlike the deterministic idea-cascades of Week 7.

✍️ worked example (one SIR run on 6 nodes, $T_I=1$)
  1. Day 0: node 1 is infected; the other 5 are susceptible.
  2. Day 1: node 1 tosses a coin for each susceptible neighbour and infects, say, nodes 3 and 5. At day's end node 1 has served its $T_I=1$ day → recovered.
  3. Day 2: nodes 3 and 5 now try their neighbours; say node 5 infects node 4 and node 3 infects no one. End of day → 3 and 5 recover.
  4. Day 3: node 4 tries its neighbours and infects no one; it recovers.
  5. Day 4: no node is infected → the process stops. Final: 4 recovered, 2 still susceptible. $$ \boxed{\text{with } T_I=2, \text{ each node instead gets two days to infect before recovering}} $$

For a disease you can catch again (common cold), there's no immunity — you go from Infected straight back to Susceptible. That's the SIS model (Susceptible → Infected → Susceptible).

  1. Which model fits measles, and which fits the common cold? Why?
    SIR for measles — one infection gives lifetime immunity (→ Recovered). SIS for the common cold — no lasting immunity, so you return to Susceptible and can catch it again.
  2. Why must an SIR process always terminate, while an SIS process might not?
    In SIR every infected node eventually becomes recovered and is permanently removed; with a finite supply of nodes, infections run out. In SIS nodes cycle back to susceptible, so infection can loop indefinitely (though it can also die out).
  3. In SIR, why can a recovered node be ignored for the rest of the simulation?
    Because it can neither infect others nor be re-infected — it's out of the dynamics entirely (immune or removed).
LECTURES 136–137

R₀ on real networks & percolation

why p·k is only a tree-truth, and a static view of the whole process

5 Two refinements

R₀ = p·k doesn't transfer to real networks. On a tree the formula is exact, but real networks have overlapping neighbourhoods (triadic closure) and recovered nodes that block re-infection — so the same $R_0$ can behave very differently.

⚠️ a counterexample the professor raises

He builds a network where every node has 2 children and $p = 2/3$, giving $R_0 = 2\times\tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{4}{3} > 1$ — which "should" mean epidemic. Yet on that particular structure the disease still dies out. Lesson: on a real network, $R_0 = p\cdot k$ (the tree formula) no longer guarantees an epidemic — you have to account for how paths overlap and how recovered nodes wall off the infection. The clean $R_0$ threshold is a property of the idealized branching/tree model (proved in §6), not of every network.

In the lecture's counterexample each edge fails to transmit with probability $1-p = 1-\tfrac23 = \tfrac13$, and he tracks the chance the infection fails to jump from one level to the next through the level's edges (e.g. $(\tfrac13)^{4}=\tfrac{1}{81}$ for four edges) — the point being that on a real network the outcome is governed by how the paths overlap, not by $R_0=p\cdot k$ alone.

Percolation — freeze the randomness

The SIR/SIS simulations run dynamically, day by day. The percolation model is a clever trick to make the whole thing static: instead of tossing a coin for an edge at the moment infection reaches it, pre-toss a coin for every edge up front.

⚙️ percolation, step by step
  1. For each edge, flip a biased coin once (head prob $p$). Head → the edge is an open pipe; tail → a closed pipe.
  2. Imagine the disease as water flowing through open pipes only.
  3. A node ends up infected iff there is a path of open pipes from the source to it. No open path ⇒ never infected.

This gives exactly the same infected set as the day-by-day SIR run, but with no notion of time — which makes it far easier to analyse. The name: the infection "percolates" through the network like water.

  1. In the percolation view, how do you tell whether a given node gets infected?
    Check whether there is a path of open pipes (edges whose pre-flipped coin came up heads) from the initially infected source to that node. If such a path exists, it's infected; otherwise not.
  2. The tree formula gives $R_0 = 4/3 > 1$ but the disease dies on a certain real network. What's the moral?
    $R_0 = p\cdot k$ is exact only for the idealized tree/branching model. On real networks, overlapping neighbourhoods and recovered nodes change the dynamics, so $R_0 > 1$ (tree sense) does not guarantee an epidemic.
LECTURES 138–142

Proof: why R₀ = 1 is the knife-edge

a recurrence, a curve, and the slope at the origin

6 The cobweb proof

Now the rigorous version (for the branching/tree model). Let $q_n$ be the probability the disease reaches level $n$ (at least one node at level $n$ is infected). We want $q^{*} = \lim_{n\to\infty} q_n$: the chance it survives forever. $q^{*} = 1$ (or just $>0$) means epidemic; $q^{*} = 0$ means it dies out.

📖 building the recurrence
  • $q_0 = 1$ — the root (level 0) is infected for sure; that's the whole setup.
  • For infection to reach level $n$ through a particular first edge, that edge must fire (prob $p$) and the infection must then travel from that child down $n-1$ more levels (prob $q_{n-1}$). So one branch succeeds with prob $p\,q_{n-1}$.
  • There are $k$ such first-edges. Level $n$ is reached iff at least one branch succeeds = $1-$(all $k$ fail).
the recurrence qₙ = f(qₙ₋₁)
$$ q_n = 1-\big(1-p\,q_{n-1}\big)^{k}, \qquad f(x)=1-(1-px)^{k} $$
read it as: "the chance of reaching the next level is 1 minus the chance that all $k$ branches fail, where one branch fails with probability $(1-p\,q_{n-1})$." Iterating this map from $q_0=1$ gives $q^{*}=f(f(f(\cdots f(1))))$ — the value we're chasing.

Study the curve $y=f(x)$ against the line $y=x$ on $[0,1]$:

cobweb: iterating f from x = 1

$R_0 > 1$: slope at origin exceeds 1, so $f$ starts above $y=x$ and the cobweb settles at a fixed point $q^{*} > 0$.

Key facts about $f$:

  • $f(0)=0$ and $f(1)=1-(1-p)^{k} < 1$.
  • Its slope at the origin is the derivative $f'(x)=k(1-px)^{k-1}\,p$, so $$f'(0)=k\cdot p = R_0.$$

So the slope of $f$ at the origin is exactly $R_0$ — that's the whole trick.

🏁 finishing with the cobweb
  • $R_0 > 1$: slope at origin $> 1$, so $f$ climbs above $y=x$ near 0 and crosses it at some point $q^{*} > 0$. Iterating $f$ from $x=1$ (bouncing vertically to the curve, horizontally to $y=x$) converges to that intersection ⇒ $q^{*} > 0$ ⇒ the disease can persist (epidemic).
  • $R_0 < 1$: slope at origin $< 1$, so $f$ stays below $y=x$ the whole way; the only fixed point is $0$. The cobweb from $x=1$ marches down to $0$ ⇒ $q^{*}=0$ ⇒ the disease dies out for certain.

That is the rigorous reason the threshold sits precisely at $R_0=1$.

$R_0 = p\cdot k$ is the slope of the level-survival curve at the origin — above 1 the disease can live, below 1 it must die.

Lower $p$ (hygiene, vaccines) or lower $k$ (quarantine) to drag $R_0$ under 1 and end the epidemic.

  1. What is $q^{*}$, and what do $q^{*}=0$ and $q^{*}>0$ mean?
    $q^{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_n$, the probability the infection survives to infinitely deep levels. $q^{*}=0$ ⇒ it dies out for sure; $q^{*}>0$ ⇒ it can persist as an epidemic.
  2. Where does $R_0$ show up in the function $f(x)=1-(1-px)^k$?
    As its slope at the origin: $f'(0)=k p = R_0$. That slope (vs the line $y=x$, slope 1) decides whether the cobweb converges to a positive fixed point ($R_0>1$) or to 0 ($R_0<1$).
  3. In the cobweb picture, why does $R_0 < 1$ force $q^{*}=0$?
    With slope $<1$ at the origin, $f$ lies entirely below $y=x$, so its only fixed point on $[0,1]$ is 0. Iterating $f$ from 1 therefore slides down to 0 — the disease dies out with probability 1.
