Week 11 — The Small-World Effect

Lectures 143–153 · six degrees of separation, Milgram's letters, the Watts–Strogatz model (homophily + weak ties), decentralized search and Kleinberg's magic exponent — then we build a small-world network in NetworkX.
🧠 the big picture, first

Take any two people on Earth — they're joined by a chain of only about six acquaintances. Milgram showed this in 1967 with forwarded letters ("six degrees of separation"). It feels impossible for 6-billion-plus people, but it isn't: with ~100 friends each, friends-of-friends explode as $100^{\text{hops}}$ and cover the world in ~10 steps. Watts & Strogatz (1998) explained the structure: most of your ties are local (homophily — a grid/ring of near neighbours), plus a few long-range weak ties. Add just a handful of long links to a ring and its diameter collapses. Even more striking, Milgram's forwarding is a decentralized (myopic) search: nobody sees the whole graph, yet greedily passing "toward" the target still finds a short path — and Kleinberg showed it works best when long links follow $1/d^{\,k}$ with $k$ = the grid's dimension ($k=2$ for a plane).

LECTURE 143

Is the world connected?

Ram in Delhi wants to reach Jennifer in Los Angeles

1 The friendship graph of the world

Back in 1970 (no internet), Ram in Delhi wants to reach Jennifer in Los Angeles, whom he doesn't know. He can't contact her directly, but maybe he knows someone who knows someone… who knows Jennifer. Model the whole world as a graph: nodes = people, an edge between two people if they know each other — the friendship graph of the world.

Two questions follow: (1) Is this graph even connected (is there a path between any two people)? (2) If so, how long is that path on average? The surprising answers: yes, it's connected — and any two people are separated by only about six hops.

📖 the chain

Ram → Mike (London) → Peter (Boston, originally from LA) → Monica (LA, Jennifer's friend) → Jennifer. Nobody knew Jennifer directly, yet each person forwarded to someone likely closer to her — and a short chain appeared. That "few hops" number, observed across the whole world, is the small-world effect.

  1. What two questions define the "small-world" problem in graph terms?
    (1) Is the world's friendship graph connected (a path between any two people)? (2) If so, what is the average distance (number of hops) between two people?
LECTURE 144

Milgram's experiment & why 6 isn't crazy

forwarded letters, and exponential reach

2 Six degrees of separation

In 1967, Milgram ran the actual experiment. He gave a letter to a random person $P_1$, who had to get it to a random target $P_2$ (whose address was written on it) — but not by mailing it directly. $P_1$ had to hand it to an acquaintance they thought was closer to $P_2$; that person did the same, and so on. Surprisingly, most letters arrived — in about six steps on average. Published in Psychology Today, this became six degrees of separation.

🤔 counterintuitive… at first

Over 6 billion people, yet only 6 hops apart? That sounds impossible — one number is huge, the other tiny. The world is big (billions of people) but small (six people connect any two).

✍️ why 6 is actually reasonable (exponential reach, lecture 144)
  1. Say you have 100 friends (level 1).
  2. Each of them has 100 friends → level 2 has $100^2 = 10{,}000$.
  3. Level 3 has $100^3$, … level $i$ has $100^{\,i}$.
  4. By level 10 that's $100^{10}$ — far more than the world's population. $$ \boxed{\text{friends-of-friends grow exponentially — 10 hops blankets the planet}} $$

So a small hop-count reaching everyone is not paradoxical — exponential growth exhausts billions in a single-digit number of levels.

  1. What was the rule Milgram's participants had to follow, and what did he find?
    Forward the letter only to a personal acquaintance you believe is closer to the target (never mail it directly). Most letters reached the target in about 6 hops on average — "six degrees of separation."
  2. Why isn't "6 hops among billions" a contradiction?
    Because reach grows exponentially: with 100 friends each, level $i$ has $100^i$ people, so ~10 levels already exceed the world population. Short chains can still cover everyone.
LECTURES 145–146

The reason: homophily + weak ties

the Watts–Strogatz model — a lattice with a few long links

3 A grid, rewired

In 1998, Watts and Strogatz redid the experiment on email networks and gave the reason. Two ingredients we've already met:

📖 the two ingredients
  • Homophily — you mostly befriend people near you (same locality, school, club). Picture people on a grid/ring, each linked to close neighbours.
  • Weak ties — a few of your friends are far away (another city, country, continent). These long-range links are the shortcuts.

Most of your edges stay inside your "territory" (homophily); a couple jump outside it (weak ties). Those couple of long jumps are what make the world small.

ring lattice + weak-tie shortcuts

grey ring = local (homophily) ties; coral chords = a few long-range weak ties.
⚙️ the Watts–Strogatz generative model
  1. Put all nodes on a cycle (ring); connect each to its nearest few neighbours (a lattice) — pure homophily.
  2. Randomly rewire a small fraction (say ~1%) of edges: pick an edge, remove it, and reconnect it to a random node — creating a long-range "teleportation" shortcut.

The result has the small-world property: high local clustering and tiny distances between any two nodes. Nobody literally "rewires" in real life — it just captures the fact that each of us has a couple of far-away friends.

  1. What two ingredients did Watts & Strogatz identify, and which one creates the shortcuts?
    Homophily (mostly local, near-neighbour ties) and weak ties (a few long-range links). The weak ties are the shortcuts that shrink distances.
  2. Describe the Watts–Strogatz generative model in one line.
    Start from a ring lattice (each node linked to nearby neighbours), then randomly rewire a small fraction of edges into long-range links — yielding short paths plus high clustering.
LECTURES 147–149

Decentralized search & Kleinberg's exponent

finding a short path with only local knowledge

4 Search with no map

The most fascinating part of Milgram's experiment isn't that a short path exists — it's that people found it without a map. Each holder of the letter knows only their own friends, not the global graph, yet by passing it to whoever seems "closest" to the target, the letter arrives. That's a decentralized search — no central authority, just local decisions.

📖 what makes it remarkable
  • It's a search algorithm running on the friendship graph.
  • It's local (greedy): each person only forwards to their best-looking neighbour — no one has the global picture.
  • Yet the collective, cooperative effort delivers the letter in ~6 hops. (Because it's short-sighted, it's also called myopic search — coded next week.)

Kleinberg's magic exponent

Does decentralized search always work well? It depends on how the long-range links are distributed. Model a person's chance of having a far friend $v$ at grid-distance $d$ as proportional to $1/d^{\,k}$, and tune the exponent $k$.

probability of a long-range link at distance d
$$ P(u \sim v)\;\propto\;\frac{1}{d(u,v)^{\,k}} $$
read it as: "the farther a node is, the less likely it's your friend — and $k$ controls how fast that drops." $k=0$ → all distances equally likely (links go anywhere — you overshoot the target); large $k$ → almost no long links (you crawl neighbour-by-neighbour). There's a sweet spot in between.
🚌 the bus-route / metro intuition

Plan a city's transit: you can't run a line between every pair of the 100 stops. If your lines are all super-long express hops ($k$ too small), you shoot past your stop and must backtrack. If they're all tiny local hops ($k$ too big), you change trains endlessly. The efficient design has routes at every scale — some short, some medium, some long — so you can always halve your remaining distance.

🏁 the result

Plot the myopic-search path length against $k$ and it bottoms out at a special value: $k = 2$ for a 2-D grid (and $k = 1$ for a 1-D line) — in general, $k$ equal to the dimension of the space. At that exponent, decentralized search is fastest (paths $\sim \log$-scale). This is Kleinberg's theorem; the full proof is optional but the take-away is: navigability needs long links spread across all distance-scales.

  1. Why is Milgram's experiment described as a "decentralized" (local) search?
    No participant knows the entire friendship graph — each only knows their own acquaintances and greedily forwards to the one seeming closest to the target. Short paths emerge from purely local decisions, with no central coordinator.
  2. If long links follow $P\propto 1/d^{k}$, what value of $k$ makes decentralized search most efficient on a 2-D grid, and what goes wrong for $k$ too small or too large?
    $k = 2$ (the grid's dimension). Too small ($k\to0$): links are uniformly long, so you overshoot and backtrack. Too large: almost no long links, so you inch along locally. $k=2$ balances links across all scales for fastest navigation.
LECTURES 150–153

Coding a small-world network

a ring, homophily ties, and a few weak ties in NetworkX

5 Building it in NetworkX

Now construct a 1-D small-world network: a ring of nodes with local (homophily) edges, plus a handful of random long-range weak ties. Start with the nodes:

ring.py — basepython
import networkx as nx
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G = nx.Graph()
G.add_nodes_from(range(50))     # 50 nodes labelled 0..49
nx.draw(G)
plt.show()

Homophily edges: connect each node to 2 neighbours on each side of the ring. The subtlety is wrap-around — node 49's "right neighbour" is node 0.

homophily edgespython
def add_homophily_edges(G):
    nodes = list(G.nodes())
    n = G.number_of_nodes()
    for i in range(n):
        # left neighbours i-1, i-2 wrap automatically (Python negative indexing)
        G.add_edge(nodes[i], nodes[i - 1])
        G.add_edge(nodes[i], nodes[i - 2])
        # right neighbours i+1, i+2 must be wrapped by hand
        for step in (1, 2):
            target = i + step
            if target > n - 1:      # went past the last node → wrap to the front
                target = target - n
            G.add_edge(nodes[i], nodes[target])
⚠️ the wrap-around bug (the one the professor hit live)
  • Left indices i-1, i-2 are safe — Python's negative indexing already wraps (nodes[-1] is the last node).
  • Right indices i+1, i+2 can exceed n-1 and cause an IndexError. Fix: if target > n-1, set target = target - n (so $n\to0$, $n{+}1\to1$).
  • Define n = G.number_of_nodes() before using it — forgetting this throws "name n is not defined."

Weak ties: add long-range links by picking two distinct random nodes and joining them. Calling it once adds one weak tie.

weak tiespython
import random

def add_long_link(G):                       # one weak tie
    v1 = random.choice(list(G.nodes()))
    v2 = random.choice(list(G.nodes()))
    while v1 == v2:                          # avoid a self-loop
        v2 = random.choice(list(G.nodes()))
    G.add_edge(v1, v2)

G = nx.Graph()
G.add_nodes_from(range(100))
add_homophily_edges(G)
for _ in range(10):                         # add 10 weak ties
    add_long_link(G)

Building the ring is O(n) (a constant number of edges per node); each weak tie is O(1). Note the professor's simplification: instead of true rewiring (delete one edge, add one), he just adds weak ties — on a big graph the edge count barely changes, but the diameter still plummets (that experiment opens Week 12).

  1. Why do the left-neighbour indices i-1, i-2 need no wrap-around fix, but i+1, i+2 do?
    Python's negative indexing already wraps (nodes[-1] is the last node), so the left side is fine. Positive indices past n-1 run off the end and raise IndexError, so you must wrap them with target = target - n.
  2. In add_long_link, what is the while v1 == v2 loop for?
    To guarantee the two randomly chosen endpoints are different, so you never add a self-loop (an edge from a node to itself).
  3. The professor adds weak ties instead of true rewiring. Why is that an acceptable shortcut?
    True rewiring deletes one edge and adds one; just adding a weak tie changes the edge count only slightly on a large graph, yet still injects the long-range shortcuts that shrink the diameter — the effect we care about.

The world is huge yet tiny: mostly-local ties plus a few long-range weak ties give short paths that even a blind, greedy search can find.
