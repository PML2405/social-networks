# Week 9 — Power Laws & the Rich-Get-Richer Phenomenon

Lectures 115–127 · why real networks are not bell curves, the preferential-attachment (Barabási–Albert) model behind them, its code, Erdős–Rényi random graphs, attack survivability, and the long tail.

🧠 the big picture, first

Heights, IQ, exam marks all pile up into a **bell curve** (a normal distribution) — because they're sums of random effects (the Central Limit Theorem). But when scientists finally measured huge real networks in the late 1990s, degree did **not** form a bell curve — it **dropped off like $1/k^{2}$**: a few nodes with enormous degree (**hubs**) and a huge crowd with tiny degree. That's a **power law**. Why? Preferential attachment: new nodes prefer to link to already-popular nodes, so the **rich get richer** — the **Barabási–Albert** model. We code it, contrast it with a purely random **Erdős–Rényi** graph (which stays a bell curve), and see the consequence for robustness: power-law networks shrug off **random** failures but shatter under a **targeted** attack on their hubs. Finally, the same maths gives the **long tail** — why niche products can out-sell the bestsellers combined.

LECTURES 115–117

## Two shapes: the bell curve vs the drop

normal distributions everywhere — then a surprise in real networks

## 1 The bell curve, and then the surprise

Walk a town and record every adult's **height**; plot height (x) against the percentage of people at that height (y). You get a hump: very few short, very few very tall (above 7 ft), most bunched around 5.3–5.4 ft. That shape — low on both sides, a peak in the middle — is the normal distribution, a.k.a. the bell curve. IQ, weight, and many natural quantities look the same.

Now do it for a **network**: plot the degree distribution — degree $k$ on x, the percentage of nodes with that degree on y. For a random graph $G(1000,\,0.1)$ (1000 nodes, each possible edge added with probability 0.1) you again get a bell curve, peaking around degree **100** = $n\cdot p = 1000\times 0.1$. No surprise.

#### bell curve vs power law

[diagram: normal | power law | (long tail →) | degree k →]

both plot degree (x) vs % of nodes (y). The power law **drops** then **extends** far right — high-degree hubs exist.

The shock came when networks got big enough to measure (WWW, phone calls, downloads). Plot the **in-degree** of web pages and you do not get a bell — you get a **drop** shaped like $1/k^{2}$: lots of pages with few in-links, and a long tail of a few pages with enormous in-degree.

📖 power law
A power-law distribution is one where $f(k)\propto 1/k^{\alpha}$ — it drops steeply but never quite hits zero, so very-high-degree nodes (hubs) genuinely exist. The bell curve has no such tail.

🌍 power laws are everywhere
- **WWW** — page in-degree $\sim 1/k^{2}$.
- **Phone calls** — call-duration counts drop off with a long tail of very long calls.
- **Song downloads** — a few songs downloaded a huge number of times.

Dozens of real datasets show it. The exponent isn't always 2 — it's typically between **2 and 3**.

- A random graph $G(1000,0.1)$ has its degree distribution peak at roughly what degree, and why?
Around **100** = $n\cdot p$. Each node has 999 possible neighbours, each attached with probability 0.1, so the expected degree is ≈ 100 and the distribution bunches (bell-shaped) around it.

- What single feature separates a power-law degree distribution from a normal one?
The **long tail**: a power law keeps a small but non-zero fraction of nodes at very high degree (hubs). A normal distribution effectively has none — nothing far from the mean.

LECTURE 116

## Why a bell curve? — the Central Limit Theorem

a sum of random things is (almost always) normal

## 2 Sums of random variables → normal

Before asking why networks are weird, understand why the bell curve is so common. Have 10 people each pick a random number from 1–100, then **add** the picks. Each single pick is uniform (any value 1–100 equally likely), but the **sum** is not:

✍️ worked example (10 random numbers, lecture 116)
- Minimum sum = all pick 1 → $10$. Maximum = all pick 100 → $1000$.
- To hit the sum **10**, every one of the 10 people must roll a 1 — extremely unlikely. Same for hitting 1000.
- A middling sum like **~550** can happen in astronomically many ways, so it's far more likely.
- Plot the sum's distribution → a bell curve peaking around **550**. $$ \boxed{\text{sum of random variables} \Rightarrow \text{normal distribution}} $$

read it as: this is the **Central Limit Theorem** — "whenever your quantity is the sum of many independent random pieces, its distribution is a bell curve." Height = sum of many genetic + environmental effects; that's why it's normal. A power law, by contrast, is not a simple sum — something else is generating it.

- Why is a sum of 10 uniform picks peaked in the middle rather than flat?
There are vastly more combinations that add to a middling total (~550) than to the extremes (10 or 1000), which each require every pick to be identical. More ways → higher probability → a central peak.

- Height follows a bell curve; page in-degree follows a power law. What does that tell you about how each is generated?
Height is a sum of many independent small factors (CLT → normal). In-degree is not a simple sum — it's produced by a growth process (preferential attachment) that concentrates links on a few nodes, giving a power law.

LECTURE 118

## Detecting a power law — the log-log litmus test

take logs, and a power law becomes a straight line

## 3 The log-log straight line

Given a network, how do you prove it's power-law (and find the exponent)? Eyeballing the drop isn't enough — many curves drop. The trick is to take logarithms.

take log of both sides of f(k)=1/kᵅ

$$ \log f(k) = \log\!\frac{1}{k^{\alpha}} = -\alpha\,\log k $$

read it as: set $y=\log f(k)$ and $x=\log k$; then $y=-\alpha\,x$ — the equation of a **straight line** through the origin with slope $-\alpha$. So if you replot the data on **log axes** and it lines up straight, it's a power law, and the slope hands you the exponent.

🧪 the litmus test
- Plot the degree distribution on ordinary axes — you see a drop, but it "could be anything".
- Redraw it as a log-log plot: $\log f(k)$ on y, $\log k$ on x.
- A straight line ⇒ power law. Its slope (in magnitude) is the exponent $\alpha$ (often 2–3).

- On a log-log plot your degree data forms a straight line of slope $-2.5$. What do you conclude?
The distribution is a power law $f(k)\propto k^{-2.5}$ — exponent $\alpha = 2.5$. The straight line on log axes is the signature; the slope magnitude is the exponent.

- Why isn't "the ordinary plot looks like a drop" enough to claim a power law?
Many different functions produce a drop (exponential, etc.). Only $1/k^{\alpha}$ becomes a straight line under log-log, so that transform is the decisive test.

LECTURES 119–120

## Preferential attachment — the rich get richer

new nodes prefer popular nodes, and a power law emerges

## 4 Attach in proportion to degree

What process produces a power law? The leading hypothesis is preferential attachment. Picture a classroom: start with 3 mutual friends. Every new arrival makes friends with a fixed number of existing people (say 2), but not at random — they're more likely to befriend someone who already has many friends (a popular person is more visible).

probability a newcomer links to node i

$$ P(\text{link to } i) = \frac{\deg(i)}{\sum_{j}\deg(j)} $$

read it as: "you attach to a node with probability equal to **its degree divided by the total degree** of everyone." A node with degree 4 is twice as likely to be chosen as a node with degree 2.

✍️ worked example (the classroom, lecture 119)
- Start: a, b, c, d with degrees $3, 2, 3, 2$ → total degree $10$. New person **e** attaches to each with probability $\tfrac{3}{10},\tfrac{2}{10},\tfrac{3}{10},\tfrac{2}{10}$ (sums to 1).
- Say e links to a and c. Update: a and c rise to degree 4; total degree becomes $14$.
- Next person **f** now sees $\tfrac{4}{14}$ for a, $\tfrac{2}{14}$ for b, $\tfrac{4}{14}$ for c, … — the already-popular a and c are even more attractive.
- Continue to 1000 nodes → the degree distribution is a **power law**. $$ \boxed{\text{high-degree nodes attract even more edges — rich get richer}} $$

💡 the punchline
A network that **grows** node-by-node while **respecting existing degrees** naturally develops hubs and a power-law tail. This is the rich-get-richer phenomenon (also called the **Matthew effect**). It's a hypothesis for why power laws appear — well-supported, not proven.

- Total degree in a network is 20. Node X has degree 5. What's the chance a newcomer links to X (per edge it forms)?
$\deg(X)/\sum\deg = 5/20 = \mathbf{0.25}$. Attachment probability is proportional to current degree.

- Why does preferential attachment produce hubs, whereas linking uniformly at random would not?
Under preferential attachment a node's chance of gaining an edge grows with the degree it already has — a positive feedback loop that lets a few nodes run away to very high degree. Uniform random linking gives every node the same chance, so no runaway hubs (→ bell curve).

LECTURES 121–122

## Coding the Barabási–Albert model

grow a network by preferential attachment, in NetworkX

## 5 Implementing rich-get-richer (BA model)

The Barabási–Albert (BA) model is exactly preferential attachment made into an algorithm. Parameters: `n` = total nodes, `m0` = size of the tiny starting network, `m` = how many existing nodes each newcomer links to (with $m \le m_0$; the code uses $m=m_0-1$).

⚙️ the algorithm, in words
- Start with an initial network of $m_0$ nodes where **every node has ≥1 edge** (a path graph works).
- Add nodes one at a time until there are $n$. For each new node, compute each existing node's probability $=\deg/\!\sum\deg$.
- Pick $m$ **distinct** targets using those probabilities and link to them.

### The clever bit — choosing by "cumulative windows"

How do you pick a node with probability proportional to its share? Lay the probabilities end-to-end on the number line $[0,1]$ as windows, roll a random $r\in[0,1]$, and see which window it lands in — a bigger share = a wider window = more likely to be hit (a **roulette wheel**).

✍️ worked example (cumulative windows, lecture 121)
- Three nodes with probabilities $0.2, 0.3, 0.5$. Cumulative list: $[0.2,\; 0.5,\; 1.0]$.
- Windows: node 1 owns $(0,0.2]$, node 2 owns $(0.2,0.5]$, node 3 owns $(0.5,1.0]$.
- Roll $r=0.4$ → it's $>0.2$ and $\le 0.5$ → falls in node 2's window → pick **node 2**.
- Node 3's window is widest (0.5), so it's picked most often — exactly proportional to probability. ✔

barabasi_albert.pypython

```
import networkx as nx
import random
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def barabasi(G, n, m0):
    m = m0 - 1                                   # edges each new node brings
    for i in range(m0 + 1, n + 1):               # add nodes one at a time
        G.add_node(i)

        # --- pre-processing: probability of each node = its share of total degree ---
        degrees = dict(nx.degree(G))             # {node: degree}
        total = float(sum(degrees.values()))
        node_prob = {v: degrees[v] / total for v in G.nodes()}

        # cumulative "windows" over [0,1], kept in order (list of [node, cum_prob])
        cum, running = [], 0.0
        for v, p in node_prob.items():
            running += p
            cum.append([v, running])

        # --- choose m DISTINCT existing nodes by their windows (roulette wheel) ---
        target_nodes, edges_added = [], 0
        while edges_added < m:
            r = random.uniform(0, 1)
            prev, k = 0.0, 0
            while not (r > prev and r <= cum[k][1]):   # find the window holding r
                prev = cum[k][1]
                k += 1
            target = cum[k][0]
            if target in target_nodes:           # already linked → try again
                continue
            target_nodes.append(target)
            G.add_edge(i, target)
            edges_added += 1
    return G

G = nx.path_graph(4)          # initial network: m0 = 4, every node has an edge
barabasi(G, 1000, 4)          # grow to 1000 nodes
```

In the screencast the professor takes `n` from the user (`raw_input`/`input`) and picks $m_0$ as a **random integer in $[2,\,n/5]$** (so $n{=}100\Rightarrow m_0\in[2,20]$) — though he notes you can just fix $m_0=4$, as above. He also reuses a `display_graph(G, i, ne)` helper each iteration to watch the network grow: it lays nodes out with `nx.circular_layout` and calls `nx.draw_networkx_nodes`/`draw_networkx_edges` separately so the **new** node/edges show green (dotted) and the existing ones red.

Plot the resulting degree distribution (count nodes at each degree) — for large `n` it's a power law; on a log-log plot it's a straight line. Complexity: for each of $n$ new nodes we rebuild probabilities in O(n) and scan windows, so the whole build is about O(n²).

⚠️ common bugs
- **Duplicate edges.** Without the `target_nodes` check, the same node can be picked twice, so a newcomer ends up with fewer than $m$ distinct neighbours. Always skip & retry.
- **Zero-degree start.** If a starting node has degree 0 its probability is 0 and it can never be chosen (and $\sum\deg$ could be 0 → division error). Use a `path_graph` so every node begins with an edge.
- **Window off-by-one.** The condition must be $\text{prev} < r \le \text{cum}[k]$ — get the boundary wrong and you pick the neighbour on the edge of the window.
- Recompute probabilities every round — degrees change as you add edges, so a stale probability table breaks the "rich get richer" feedback.

- In the roulette-wheel method, why is a high-probability node chosen more often?
Its cumulative window on $[0,1]$ is wider, so a uniform random $r$ is more likely to land inside it. Window width = the node's probability.

- You run BA but forget to track already-chosen targets. What symptom appears?
Some new nodes get fewer than $m$ edges, because the same target can be selected repeatedly and the duplicate is silently dropped — distorting the degree distribution.

LECTURES 123–124

## Erdős–Rényi — a truly random graph

flip a coin for every possible edge → a bell curve, not a power law

## 6 The G(n, p) random graph

The Erdős–Rényi model $G(n,p)$ is the "no preference" baseline: take $n$ nodes and, for every one of the $\binom{n}{2}$ possible pairs, add an edge with probability $p$ (toss a biased coin). Higher $p$ ⇒ more edges; $p=0$ ⇒ no edges, $p=1$ ⇒ complete graph.

erdos_renyi.pypython

```
import networkx as nx
import random

def erdos_renyi(G, p):
    for i in G.nodes():
        for j in G.nodes():
            if i != j:                     # no self-loops
                r = random.random()        # uniform in [0,1)
                if r <= p:
                    G.add_edge(i, j)       # (i,j) == (j,i) in an undirected graph
    return G

G = nx.Graph()
n = int(raw_input("n: "))                  # Python 3: input(...)
p = float(raw_input("p: "))
G.add_nodes_from(range(n))                 # n isolated nodes to start
erdos_renyi(G, p)
```

Its degree distribution is a **bell curve** (each node's degree is a sum of $n-1$ independent coin flips — CLT again — with mean $(n-1)p$). No preferential attachment ⇒ no hubs ⇒ no power law. Building it touches every ordered pair, so it's O(n²).

Two handy shortcuts from the screencast: NetworkX has a **built-in** `nx.erdos_renyi_graph(n, p)` that returns a random graph directly (the professor uses it to make the random network for the attack test below), and he reuses a `plot_degree_distribution(G)` helper — take all degrees via `nx.degree(G).values()`, form the **sorted list of unique degrees**, then for each one `all_degrees.count(k)` gives the y-value (number of nodes at degree $k$).

| | Barabási–Albert (BA) | Erdős–Rényi $G(n,p)$ |
| how edges form | growth + preferential attachment (∝ degree) | each pair independently, fixed prob $p$ |
| hubs? | yes — a few very-high-degree nodes | no — degrees cluster near the mean |
| degree distribution | power law ($1/k^{\alpha}$) | normal (bell curve) |
| models… | real-world networks (WWW, social) | a pure-chance null model |

- Why does $G(n,p)$ give a bell curve while BA gives a power law?
In $G(n,p)$ each node's degree is a sum of independent coin flips (CLT ⇒ normal, no preference). BA keeps feeding edges to already-popular nodes, creating a heavy tail of hubs (power law).

- In $G(100, 0.5)$, roughly what is a typical node's degree?
About $(n-1)p = 99\times 0.5 \approx 50$. Degrees bunch around this mean.

LECTURE 125

## Attack survivability — random vs targeted removal

why hubs make a network robust to accidents but fragile to attack

## 7 Knock out nodes until it breaks

Take a network and delete nodes until it becomes **disconnected**, counting how many deletions it takes. Two strategies: remove nodes **randomly** (a failure), or remove the **highest-degree** node each time (a targeted attack).

attack_survivability.pypython

```
import networkx as nx
import random

def remove_random_node(G):
    G.remove_node(random.choice(list(G.nodes())))
    return G

def remove_high_degree_node(G):
    deg = dict(G.degree())                              # {node: degree}
    nodes = sorted(deg.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
    G.remove_node(nodes[0][0])                          # the top-degree node
    return G

G = nx.read_edgelist("email.txt")     # a real-world (power-law) network
print(nx.info(G))                     # ~1000+ nodes, ~5000+ edges

# strategy 1: random failure
G1, removed_random = G.copy(), 0
while nx.is_connected(G1):
    G1 = remove_random_node(G1); removed_random += 1

# strategy 2: targeted attack on hubs
G2, removed_targeted = G.copy(), 0
while nx.is_connected(G2):
    G2 = remove_high_degree_node(G2); removed_targeted += 1

print(removed_random, removed_targeted)
```

📊 real-world (power-law) network
- Targeted (remove hubs): disconnects in ≈ **1** removal.
- Random: needs ≈ **13–17** removals.

The hubs hold the whole thing together — pull one and it falls apart.

📊 random $G(n,p)$ network (1000 nodes)
- Random removal: ≈ **985** nodes.
- Targeted removal: ≈ **928** nodes.

Barely different — there are **no hubs**, so no node is special.

### Power-law networks are robust to random failure but fragile to a targeted attack on their hubs.

An attacker who knows the degrees (e.g. seeding a virus) hits the hubs; random damage mostly wastes itself on low-degree nodes.

- Why does targeted removal shatter a real network in ~1 step but random removal doesn't?
Real networks are held together by a few high-degree hubs. Removing a hub cuts many connections at once (fast disconnection); a random node is almost always low-degree and its loss barely matters.

- On a random $G(n,p)$ graph, random and targeted removal need roughly the same number of deletions. What does that reveal?
That $G(n,p)$ has **no hubs** — degrees are all near the mean, so no single node is critical. "Targeting the highest degree" isn't meaningfully better than picking at random.

LECTURES 126–127

## A possible reason + the long tail

how the rich got rich in the first place, and why niche beats the bestseller

## 8 Path dependence, and the long tail

Preferential attachment says the rich get richer — but how did a node get rich first? If you "rewound history," would Harry Potter still be the bestseller, would the same songs still win? A famous experiment tested this.

🎵 the parallel-worlds music experiment
Put the same **48 songs** on several separate download portals (parallel worlds), each seen by a different random crowd, and **show each song's download count**. Result: a different song won in each world. Early, essentially random, popularity got **displayed**, which biased newcomers toward it, which raised its count further — a feedback loop. In a control world where counts were hidden, the winners changed again.

So success is partly **luck amplified by visibility** (path dependence). The professor's **Quora** example: the top-upvoted answer is shown first, gets more views, gets more upvotes — even a genuinely better late answer stays buried. Two honest **open questions** remain: (1) is rich-get-richer really why power laws appear (still a hypothesis)? (2) how do you subtract out popularity-noise to recover an item's true quality?

### The long tail (and Zipf's law)

Rank products by popularity (x) and plot sales (y): a power-law drop. Why does a bookstore stock 5000 titles instead of just the 10% bestsellers? Because of the long tail.

✍️ worked example (head vs tail, lecture 127)
- Total sales = area under the rank-vs-sales curve.
- The **head** (top few bestsellers) is tall but narrow — often only ~**20%** of total sales.
- The **tail** (the many niche titles) is short but very long — often ~**80%** of total sales.
- So discarding the niche 90% would throw away most of the revenue. $$ \boxed{\text{the long tail can outweigh the bestsellers combined}} $$

read it as: "popular products are few but loud; niche products are many but quiet — and together the quiet many can dominate." The same shape appears in YouTube views, and in **Zipf's law**: the $k$-th most frequent English word appears with frequency $\propto 1/k$ ("the" tops it). The long, low tail holds a huge share of the total.

One more subtlety the professor stresses: the **shape** is preserved over time even as individual items move along it. Every bestseller has a **shelf life** — after its months at the head it slides down into the niche tail (becoming a "classic"), while fresh hits keep entering the head. The exact analogy holds for network nodes: a popular node need not stay popular; it too can drift toward the low-degree tail with time.

- What is the "long tail," and why can't a big store stock only bestsellers?
The long, low part of a popularity curve — the many niche items each selling a little. Summed, that tail can be ~80% of total sales, so dropping it (keeping only the ~20% head) would lose most of the revenue.

- The music experiment gave different winners in each parallel world. What does that show about "the rich get richer"?
Which item becomes a hit is partly **luck amplified by displayed popularity** (path dependence) — not purely intrinsic quality. Showing early counts biases later choosers, so different worlds crown different winners.

- State Zipf's law for word frequency.
The frequency of the $k$-th most common word is proportional to $1/k$ — the most frequent word ("the") is used about twice as often as the 2nd, three times the 3rd, and so on. It's a power law with exponent 1.
