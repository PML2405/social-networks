Comprehension 1 — Seeding a Campaign: Cores and Going Viral
Case Study
A social-media growth team, led by Omar, wants a campaign clip to go viral. Anything that spreads across
the internet — a picture, a video, a slogan — they loosely call an internet meme, and they know only a tiny
fraction of clips ever catch on. Three levers shape whether a clip spreads: the quality of the clip itself, the
structure of the platform's follower network (which is fixed and the same for everyone), and the choice of
which accounts to seed it with. Since they cannot rewrite the network, their effort goes into picking the
right accounts to infect.
Which accounts are the “right” ones? Candidates include the highest-degree account, the most central by
closeness, or the best connector by betweenness. The team learns that the most reliable target is the
network's core. Many real networks have a core–periphery shape: a small, densely interconnected core
surrounded by a sparsely connected periphery. Seed one core account and, because the core is so tightly
knit, the idea races through the whole core and from there out to the periphery.
To locate the core precisely the team uses the notion of a k-core — a subnetwork whose members each
retain at least k connections — and the related core number of each account, the largest k for which it still
belongs to the k-core. Comparing accounts, they find that this coreness lines up with real spreading power
better than degree, closeness, or betweenness do. They also discover a practical shortcut: certain accounts
that sit just outside the innermost core turn out to spread a clip just as far, which spares them from having
to reach the hardest-to-contact celebrities.

Comprehension 2 — Peeling the Network: k-Shell Decomposition
Case Study
The same team implements k-shell decomposition to find the core and to rank accounts by influence. The
procedure peels the network in rounds. With an empty bucket labelled 1, it removes every node of degree
1; pulling a node out also strips away its edges, so a neighbour can lose enough links to fall to degree 1 as
well, and those newly-exposed nodes are removed into the same bucket until none of degree 1 remain. The
bucket is set aside, a new bucket labelled 2 is opened, and nodes of degree 2 or less are peeled the same
way, and so on, until the network is empty.
An important subtlety is that the buckets are shells, not the cores themselves. The k-core is recovered as the
union of all buckets from k upward, because every node in a higher shell trivially satisfies the lower-degree
requirement. The nodes removed in the very last bucket form the innermost shell — the core. NetworkX
exposes this directly through core numbers, and the team confirms that ranking accounts by core number
tracks their cascade influence more closely than degree, closeness, or betweenness.
Finally, to estimate each shell's reach, the team runs an independent-cascade simulation many times from
seeds drawn out of that shell and averages how many accounts end up infected — the shell's cascade
capacity. Plotting cascade capacity against shell number, they see it rise and then level off well before the
innermost shell, which is exactly why some outer-shell (pseudo-core) accounts spread a clip as far as the
core. Key fragments:
import networkx as nx​
 ​
# core number of every node (runs k-shell decomposition internally)​
cn = nx.core_number(G)​
 ​
# one peeling round into the bucket labelled k​
while any(G.degree(v) <= k for v in G.nodes):​
    low = [v for v in G.nodes if G.degree(v) <= k]​
    bucket_k.extend(low)​
    G.remove_nodes_from(low)        # deleting edges may expose new low-degree nodes
Questions 1–10
Q1.  [MCQ]  In the k-shell decomposition, what does each peeling round do?
A.  Removes only the single highest-degree node and stops the round immediately.
B.  Removes a fixed number of nodes chosen at random, regardless of their degree.
C.  Recursively removes nodes of degree k or less until none remain, then moves to k+1.
D.  Adds new nodes of degree k to the network until the graph stops growing.
Answer:  C
Reference:  Lecture 162 – Finding the right key nodes (≈04:39–end)

[p.6]
Solution:  Each round recursively peels away nodes of degree k or less until none are left, then the
threshold k increases by one. It is not random removal, not a single high-degree node, and it removes rather
than adds nodes.

Q2.  [MCQ]  Why can removing one low-degree node expose more nodes to remove in the same round?
A.  Removing a node has no effect on any other node's degree, so nothing new is exposed.
B.  Removing a node randomly raises some neighbours' degrees, which must then be removed too.
C.  Removing a node adds new edges elsewhere, lowering far-away nodes below the threshold.
D.  Removing a node also deletes its edges, which can drop a neighbour's degree below the threshold.
Answer:  D
Reference:  Lecture 162 – Finding the right key nodes (≈04:39–end)
Solution:  Deleting a node also deletes its incident edges, so a neighbour's degree can fall to or below the
current threshold and must be peeled in the same bucket. Removal lowers neighbours' degrees, does not
raise them, adds no edges, and certainly does affect neighbours.

Steps (in scrambled order):

(ii)  Repeatedly remove every node of degree ≤ k into the current bucket.
(iii) When no node of degree ≤ k remains, set the bucket aside.
(iv)  Increase k by one and open a new bucket.
(v)   Stop when the network is empty; the last bucket is the innermost shell (core).
A.  i → ii → iii → iv → v
B.  ii → i → iii → iv → v
C.  i → iii → ii → iv → v
D.  i → ii → iv → iii → v

[p.7]
Answer:  A (i → ii → iii → iv → v)
Reference:  Lecture 162 – Finding the right key nodes (k-shell)
Solution:  Begin at k = 1 with an empty bucket (i), peel all nodes of degree ≤ k into it (ii), set the bucket

Q5.  [MCQ]  After the decomposition, which nodes form the core of the network?
A.  The nodes peeled in the very first bucket, the outermost shell of the network.
B.  A random selection of nodes taken evenly from across all of the buckets.
C.  The nodes peeled in the very last bucket, the innermost shell of the network.
D.  Every node that was ever placed in any bucket during the whole procedure.
Answer:  C
Reference:  Lecture 162 – Finding the right key nodes (≈09:09–end)
Solution:  The core is the innermost shell — the nodes removed in the last bucket, which survived every
earlier peeling round. The first bucket is the outermost shell, and the core is neither a random sample nor
all bucketed nodes.

Q6.  [Statement type]  Consider the two statements about k-shell decomposition and the k-core, and decide

Statement I: Peeling a low-degree node can drop a neighbour below the threshold, exposing it for removal.
Statement II: The k-core is exactly the single bucket k, excluding all the higher-numbered buckets.
A.  Both statements are true.
B.  Both statements are false.

Answer:  C

Solution:  The first statement is correct: deleting a node's edges can push a neighbour below the threshold.
