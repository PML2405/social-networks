Comprehension 1 — Reading and Measuring Real-World Networks
Case Study
Tomás works in a lab that studies how real systems hold together. From public data portals he pulls four
networks, each saved in a different file convention: an air-traffic network whose nodes are airports and
whose links are non-stop routes (a plain two-column text file); a food web whose nodes are species and
whose links record who preys on whom (GraphML); a national power grid whose nodes are substations
joined by transmission lines (Pajek); and a research co-authorship network whose nodes are scientists,
joined whenever they have written a paper together (GML).
He is careful about direction. Some links are inherently mutual — two airports joined by a route, two
substations joined by a line, two scientists who co-authored a paper — and are modelled as undirected.
Others are inherently one-way — a predator feeding on its prey — and must be modelled as directed. He
reads each file with the NetworkX reader that fits its format, skims the headline counts, then asks the same
structural questions of every graph: how lopsided the per-node link counts are, how full the graph is against
the most links it could carry, how tightly a node’s neighbours connect among themselves, and how many
hops separate the two most distant nodes.
To keep his hand calculations honest, Tomás sketches one tiny relay network on paper — eight stations
labelled A through H, joined by exactly these nine links:
A–B,  A–C,  A–D,  B–C,  D–E,  E–F,  F–G,  G–H,  E–G
He expects each real network’s link-count profile to be lopsided in the familiar way — a few heavily
connected hubs and a long tail of lightly connected nodes.

Q1.  [MCQ]  Among Tomás’s four networks, which one must be modelled as a directed graph, and why?
A.  The air-traffic network, because each route serves two airports.
B.  The power-grid network, because a transmission line connects two substations.
C.  The food web, because “species X preys on species Y” does not imply “Y preys on X” — the relation
runs one way.
D.  The co-authorship network, because two scientists either have or have not written a paper together.
Answer:  C
Reference:  Lecture 19 – Datasets: Different Formats (≈01:31, directed vs undirected)
Solution:  A relation needs a directed edge when it is one-way. Predation is one-way: X preying on Y says
nothing about Y preying on X, so the food web is directed (C). The other three describe mutual, symmetric
relations — a shared route, a shared line, a shared paper — which are modelled with undirected edges.

Q6.  [MCQ]  For that same eight-station sketch (8 nodes, 9 links), what does nx.density return, and what
would density be for an empty graph and for a complete graph?
A.  9/28 ≈ 0.32; density is 0 for an empty graph and 1 for a complete graph.
B.  9/8 ≈ 1.13; density is always greater than 1.
C.  9/64 ≈ 0.14; density is 1 for an empty graph and 0 for a complete graph.
D.  8/9 ≈ 0.89; density equals the average degree.
Answer:  A
Reference:  Lecture 21 – Datasets: Analyzing Using NetworkX (≈27:27–30:40)
Solution:  Density is the fraction of possible edges that are present: edges / C(n,2). Here that is 9 / [8·7/2] =

C(n,2) edges are present.

Answer:  B

Comprehension 2 — How Many Links Until the Sensor Mesh Connects?
Case Study
A field-ecology team scatters 50 solar-powered sensors across a nature reserve. At first every sensor stands
alone. Over the following weeks, pairs of sensors that happen to fall within radio range of one another
switch on a link, essentially at random, one pair at a time. The team needs to know how many such links
must appear before the mesh becomes fully connected — the point at which a reading taken anywhere can
hop, link by link, all the way to the base station. Maya studies this with a probability argument and a short
simulation.
She first reasons about why the mesh almost never ends up split into two separate clusters with no link
between them. For that to happen, every one of the many possible links that could bridge the two clusters
would have to be missing at the same time. With so many independent chances to bridge the gap, all of
them failing is about as plausible as tossing a fair coin a few hundred times and never once seeing heads —
conceivable, but not something that happens in practice. So as links accumulate, a permanent split
becomes vanishingly unlikely.
To pin down how many links are actually needed, Maya borrows the lecture’s tactic of tracking the last
“lonely” sensor — one that still has no link at all. A single new link involves any one particular sensor only
with a small chance, and a sensor stays lonely only if every link formed so far has avoided it. Multiplying
those chances together and leaning on a standard calculus limit, she estimates the number of random links
at which lonely sensors essentially disappear — and, once none are left, a split-off cluster is itself extremely
unlikely, so the mesh is almost surely whole.
Finally she codes a simulation, connectivity.py. It starts from a set of unlinked sensors, repeatedly switches
on one random link between two distinct sensors until NetworkX reports the mesh is connected, then
returns how many links were needed. By averaging this over many runs and many mesh sizes, she means to
compare how the link count grows against a candidate formula. The core routines are:

Q2.  [MCQ]  Suppose the mesh threatened to split into a cluster of 30 sensors and a cluster of 20, with no
link across. How many across-cluster links are possible, and how does that compare with the total number
of possible links?
A.  50 — one per sensor.
B.  600 — close to half of all 1225 possible links.
C.  1225 — all of the possible links.
D.  10 — the difference between the cluster sizes.
Answer:  B
Reference:  Lecture 23 – Emergence of Connectedness (≈05:12)
Solution:  Each of the 30 sensors in one cluster could link to each of the 20 in the other, giving 30 × 20 = 600

Q4.  [MCQ]  In Maya’s argument, one new link joins two of the n sensors. What is the probability that this
single link avoids a particular sensor v, and the probability that v is still lonely after K independent links?
A.  Avoids with probability 2/n; lonely after K links with probability (2/n)^K.
B.  Avoids with probability 1 − 2/n; lonely after K links with probability (1 − 2/n)^K.
C.  Avoids with probability 1/n; lonely after K links with probability (1/n)^K.
D.  Avoids with probability 1 − 2/n; lonely after K links with probability K × (1 − 2/n).
Answer:  B
Reference:  Lecture 24 – Advanced Material: Emergence of Connectedness (≈11:01–13:06)
Solution:  A link picks 2 of the n sensors, so the chance it lands on v is 2/n and the chance it avoids v is 1 −
2/n. Because the K links are independent, the avoid-chances multiply: P(v still lonely after K links) = (1 −
2/n)^K. Probabilities multiply, not add, ruling out D.
