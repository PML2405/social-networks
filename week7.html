Week 7 — Cascading Behaviour & Diffusion

Lectures 87–100 · why we follow each other, how a new idea diffuses (or stalls) through a network, and a NetworkX simulation of four cascade ideas.
🧠 the big picture, first

We follow each other — for direct benefit and for information. When a new behaviour (an app, a product, an idea) spreads person-to-person it branches outward — that's diffusion. We model each choice as a coordination game: adopt the new action once a fraction q = b/(a+b) of your partners have. Diffusion doesn't always win: dense clusters block it — precisely, a cluster of density > 1−q stops a complete cascade. To get things moving, raise the payoff or seed key (well-connected) people. We then code all four ideas in NetworkX.

LECTURES 87–88

We follow — and why

conformity, direct benefit, and information

1 Do we follow? Why?

In a restaurant where everyone orders the burger, you probably order the burger too; at a fork where everyone turns right, you turn right. In the classic elevator experiment, a lone rider turns to face the back wall just because everyone else does — our sense of conformity. But following isn't always wise (if everyone's answer is 30, you may cross out your correct 40). So why do we follow?

📖 two reasons we follow
  • Direct benefit — an explicit gain: "refer my shoes to 5 friends and get a free pair," so you adopt.
  • Informational gain — you follow to get useful information: a farmer learns of better hybrid seeds from a friend; a doctor learns of a new vaccine from colleagues.
LECTURES 89–91

Diffusion & the coordination game

a behaviour branching through a network — and the switching threshold

2 Modelling how a behaviour spreads

diffusion branches (it doesn't just travel)

One adopter seeds several, who each seed several — it fans out, not a single chain.
📖 diffusion

Diffusion is the branching spread of a behaviour/idea through a network: an adopter passes it to several partners, who pass it on further. "Travel" is a misnomer — a single path — whereas diffusion fans out. The key question: how many end up adopting?

The coordination game

Each node chooses one of two actions, A (new) or B (old). You get payoff a for each partner who also does A, and b for each partner who also does B. If a fraction p of your partners have adopted A:

payoffs
$$ \text{adopt A}: p\,a \qquad \text{adopt B}: (1-p)\,b $$
switch to A when…
$$ p\,a \ge (1-p)\,b \;\Longrightarrow\; p \ge \frac{b}{a+b} $$
read it as: "adopt the new action A once at least a fraction q = b/(a+b) of your partners already have." Rearrange $pa \ge (1-p)b$: $pa \ge b - pb$, so $p(a+b) \ge b$, giving $p \ge b/(a+b)$. This q is the node's threshold.
✍️ worked examples — the threshold
  1. a = 3 (app), b = 2 (cash): $q = \dfrac{2}{3+2} = \dfrac{2}{5} = \boxed{0.4}$.
  2. A vendor has 20 partners: 6 on the app, 14 on cash. App payoff = 6·3 = 18; cash payoff = 14·2 = 28. Since 28 > 18 → stay on cash. (Equivalently p = 6/20 = 0.3 < 0.4.)
📖 social reinforcement & the three outcomes

Social reinforcement: each additional partner who adopts raises p, nudging you past your threshold. Across the whole network, a diffusion settles into one of three outcomes: everyone on A, everyone on B, or a split into two groups (one on A, one on B).

LECTURE 92

Communities, key people & density

why a cascade stalls at a community boundary

3 Communities resist; key people help

a cascade fills one community, can't cross

The new action fills the seeded community, but a single weak tie can't carry it across.
⚙️ key people & community density
  • A cascade fills the community it starts in but often can't cross a weak tie into another (the far endpoint has too many old-behaviour friends).
  • Key people: instead of advertising to everyone, seed a few well-connected nodes so the idea ripples out (viral marketing — convince the "head of the family," or put a celebrity on the box).
  • Community density = edges / C(n,2). Higher density → harder to inject a new idea (a tight bundle of sticks won't break) — tightly-knit friends resist.
LECTURE 93

Cascades and clusters

the theorem: a dense enough cluster blocks a complete cascade

4 When does a cascade fail to complete?

📖 cluster of density D · complete cascade
  • A cluster of density D: every node in it has at least a fraction D of its neighbours inside the cluster.
  • A complete cascade: every node in the network eventually adopts the new action A.
the blocking theorem
A cascade with threshold q cannot complete
if the network has a cluster of density > 1 − q.
(and the converse holds too)
read it as: "if a tight cluster keeps more than a fraction (1−q) of everyone's friends inside it, then fewer than q of their friends are outside adopters — so their threshold q is never met, and the new action can't get in."
✍️ worked example — why density > 1−q blocks

q = b/(a+b) with a=3, b=2 → q = 0.4, so 1−q = 0.6. Take a cluster of density > 0.6: node 8 has 6 neighbours, 4 inside → 4/6 ≈ 0.67 > 0.6. So >60% of its friends are inside (still on B); <40% could be outside adopters — below the 0.4 threshold. The node never switches. Same for every cluster member → the cascade can't enter → not complete.

LECTURE 94

Collective action & knowledge

how incomplete knowledge stalls a feasible protest

5 Thresholds for joining in

📖 intrinsic threshold (collective action)

In a protest/revolt, each person has an intrinsic threshold = the minimum number of participants (including themselves) that must be in before they'll join. You know your own threshold and your friends' — but not the whole network's.

✍️ worked example — a protest that never starts

Three people with thresholds u=2, v=3, w=4 — but w has only 2 friends. w can never reach 4 → w won't join. v sees w won't join, so v's threshold of 3 fails → v drops out. Then u (needs 2) sees neither will join → u drops out. No protest — even though one was in principle possible. Incomplete knowledge of others' thresholds stalls a feasible protest.

This is why firms limit cross-group communication ("divide and conquer") — isolated clusters can't coordinate a revolt.

The cascade simulation

base code + four big ideas, in NetworkX

6 Coding the cascade

cascade.py — base codepython
import networkx as nx, random
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# build ONCE and reuse, so every experiment runs on the SAME graph (fair comparison)
G = nx.erdos_renyi_graph(10, 0.5)
nx.write_gml(G, 'random_graph.gml')
G = nx.read_gml('random_graph.gml')

a, b = 4, 3                                    # payoff of A (new), of B (old)

def set_all_B(G):
    for n in G.nodes():
        G.node[n]['action'] = 'B'             # everyone starts on the old action

def set_A(G, seeds):
    for n in seeds:
        G.node[n]['action'] = 'A'             # seed nodes flip to the new action

def find_neighbours(c, node, G):              # count neighbours currently on action c
    num = 0
    for nb in G.neighbors(node):
        if G.node[nb]['action'] == c:
            num += 1
    return num

def recalculate_options(G):
    dict1 = {}
    for node in G.nodes():
        num_A = find_neighbours('A', node, G)     # A-neighbours
        num_B = find_neighbours('B', node, G)     # B-neighbours
        payoff_A = a * num_A
        payoff_B = b * num_B
        dict1[node] = 'A' if payoff_A >= payoff_B else 'B'   # adopt the higher-payoff action
    return dict1                               # everyone's next action, decided together

def reset_node_attributes(G, dict1):
    for node in dict1:
        G.node[node]['action'] = dict1[node]  # apply ALL decisions at once (synchronous)

def terminate_1(c, G):                         # returns 1 iff EVERY node holds action c
    for n in G.nodes():
        if G.node[n]['action'] != c:
            return 0
    return 1

# --- the cascade loop ---
set_all_B(G)
set_A(G, [3, 7])                               # A=green, B=red in the drawing
count = 0
while True:
    dict1 = recalculate_options(G)
    reset_node_attributes(G, dict1)
    count += 1
    if terminate_1('A', G) or terminate_1('B', G) or count >= 100:
        break                                  # all-A, all-B, or the 100-round cap

complete = terminate_1('A', G)                 # 1 = COMPLETE cascade (every node on A)
⚙️ the pipeline & key facts
  1. Build an ER graph, set every node to B → seed a few nodes to A → each round recalculate every node's action from neighbours' payoffs → stop when settled (all A / all B) or after 100 rounds → report whether the cascade is complete.
  2. num_A = a node's count of A-neighbours (not its degree, not the payoff a).
  3. Adopt A when a·num_A >= b·num_B (weighted payoffs, not a bare count).
  4. The 100-round cap stops runs where nodes flip forever (no convergence). Reaching 100 rounds ≠ a complete cascade.

⚠️ code notes
  • Decisions are synchronous: recalculate_options computes everyone's next action from the current snapshot, then reset_node_attributes applies them together.
  • Reuse the saved graph (write_gml/read_gml) across all four experiments so differences are due to the change tested, not a fresh random graph.
  • Idea 3's graph: two 10-node communities built as separate random graphs (each edge with prob 0.5, like a mini Erdős–Rényi), joined by a single link (e.g. G.add_edge(5, 15)) — then saved for reuse.
  • Idea 4's cluster: node 8 has 6 neighbours, 4 inside → 0.67 > 1−q = 0.6, so the cluster is impenetrable. Seeds are always chosen outside nodes 7–11, and the cascade never reaches them.

Diffusion branches through the network. Raise the payoff or seed key (well-connected) people to get it moving; dense communities resist. Simulate: set all B → seed A → each round adopt the higher-payoff action (a·num_A vs b·num_B) → stop at all-A / all-B / 100 rounds.

  1. Why is the spread called diffusion, not travel?
    It branches — each adopter can seed several others — rather than following a single chain or a central broadcast.
  2. A vendor switches when p·a ≥ (1−p)·b. What threshold on p?
    $p \ge \dfrac{b}{a+b}$. With a=3, b=2 → 2/5 = 0.4.
  3. a=3, b=2; 20 partners, 6 app, 14 cash — which action?
    App = 6·3 = 18; cash = 14·2 = 28 → stay on cash (p = 0.3 < 0.4).
  4. What is social reinforcement?
    Each extra partner who adopts nudges the vendor closer to (past) its threshold — the cumulative pull of peers.
  5. Match: diffusion / complete cascade / cluster of density D / key people.
    Branching spread / every node adopts A / every node has ≥ D fraction of neighbours inside / well-connected seeds.
  6. When can a cascade with threshold q not complete?
    When the network has a cluster of density > 1−q — its members have >1−q friends inside, so <q outside adopters, threshold never met.
  7. What's the idea behind seeding key people?
    Convince a few highly-connected nodes whose adoption ripples out (viral marketing) — far stronger than blanket advertising or seeding low-degree nodes.
  8. What is a vendor's intrinsic threshold (collective action)?
    The minimum number of participants (including itself) that must join before it will.
  9. Thresholds 2, 3, 4, but the "4" has only 2 partners — why no protest?
    The one needing 4 can never reach it → won't join; the "3" sees that and drops; then the "2" drops. Incomplete knowledge stalls a feasible protest.
  10. Why save/reload the ER graph with GML?
    So all four experiments use the identical graph — differences come from the change tested, not a new random graph.
  11. In recalculate_options, when does a node adopt A?
    When a·num_A >= b·num_B (its weighted A-payoff ≥ B-payoff). num_A = count of A-neighbours.
  12. Why the count >= 100 stop condition?
    To cap runs where nodes flip forever (never converge). Reaching 100 rounds does not mean the cascade completed.
