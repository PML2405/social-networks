🧠 the big picture, first

Why does your parents' advice "watch your company" hold? Because traits — even obesity and happiness — spread through friendship networks like contagions (up to 3 levels out). Two forces shape who befriends whom: selection (you pick friends already like you) and social influence (you grow like the friends you pick). The tendency of similar people to link is homophily, which we measure as 1 − actual/expected cross-group edges. Friendships also form through shared foci (clubs, gyms) — giving triadic, foci & membership closure. Finally we build all of this into one running simulation: the Fatman Evolutionary Model.

LECTURE 41

Should you watch your company?

obesity and happiness are contagious — up to three levels

1 Traits spread through the network

"Tell me who your friends are and I'll tell you who you are." A 2007 study by Christakis and Fowler gave this teeth: tracking ~5000 people over ~12 years, they found obesity is contagious — if your friends become obese, your risk rises — and, even more strikingly, happiness is contagious up to three levels out: your happiness is linked to your friends', their friends', and their friends'.

📖 contagion in a social network

A contagion is a trait/behaviour that spreads along friendship edges rather than staying with one person. It means we don't just connect — we permeate each other's states. Both obesity and happiness were shown to spread this way (happiness to 3 levels).

LECTURES 42–43

Selection vs Social Influence

did you pick a similar friend, or grow similar after? — the Wikipedia study

2 Two forces behind every friendship

📖 selection vs social influence
  • Selection — you befriend people already similar to you (similarity comes first). E.g. two French-speakers become friends; same-social-status people befriend; a class topper's friends are toppers.
  • Social influence — you become similar to friends after befriending them (friendship comes first). E.g. you start smoking / eating junk / partying because a friend does.

The similarity measure

similarity of two people
$$ \text{sim}(A,B)=\frac{|\,\text{interests}(A)\cap\text{interests}(B)\,|}{|\,\text{interests}(A)\cup\text{interests}(B)\,|} $$
read it as: "shared interests ÷ all distinct interests between them." (Same shape as neighbourhood overlap, but over interests/edited-pages, not friends.)
✍️ worked example — Anita & Bina

Anita = {chess, hiking, jazz}; Bina = {hiking, jazz, baking, cycling}. Shared = {hiking, jazz} = 2; union = {chess, hiking, jazz, baking, cycling} = 5. $$\text{sim}=\frac{2}{5}=\boxed{0.4}.$$

similarity of two Wikipedia editors around first contact

Average similarity climbs before first contact (selection — similar people choose to interact) and climbs further after (influence). On Wikipedia the pre-contact (selection) rise is steeper.
⚙️ how the Wikipedia study separates the two

Plot average editor-similarity vs time around their first talk-page conversation (t=0). The rise before t=0 = selection; the rise after = influence. On Wikipedia selection dominated; on obesity data the reverse held (more influence). So which force wins is context-dependent.

LECTURE 44

Homophily — definition & measurement

a coin-toss baseline, then 1 − actual/expected

3 Measuring homophily

📖 homophily

Homophily = "birds of a feather flock together" — similar people befriend one another more than chance predicts. First ask does the network show it (yes/no), then how much.

The chance baseline. At a party of 50 teenagers + 50 middle-aged, picking a random friendship is like tossing two coins: two teens = ¼, two middle-aged = ¼, one of each (cross-group) = ½. So half the friendships should be cross-group by chance. If far fewer are cross-group → homophily.

homophily measure
$$ H = 1 - \frac{\text{actual cross-group edges}}{\text{expected cross-group edges}} $$
read it as: "how far below chance is the number of cross-group friendships." H > 0 = homophily (fewer cross ties than chance); H = 0 = chance-level/random mixing; H < 0 = heterophily (more cross ties than chance — e.g. an actors-vs-actresses party).
✍️ worked examples — homophily value
  1. Club of 20 (10 first-years + 10 final-years), 30 friendships, 9 cross-year. 50–50 split → expected cross = ½·30 = 15. $$H = 1 - \frac{9}{15} = 1 - 0.6 = \boxed{0.4}\ (\text{positive} \Rightarrow \text{homophily}).$$
  2. Transcript's class of 10 (4 girls, 6 boys), 18 edges, 3 boy–girl. Expected cross = ½·18 = 9. $H = 1 - \frac{3}{9} = 1 - 0.33 = 0.67$ (strong homophily).

The ½ is exact only for a 50–50 split; for other splits use the appropriate expected-cross fraction. The measure isn't limited to 50–50.

LECTURE 45

Three kinds of closure

triadic · foci · membership

4 How friendships (and memberships) form

triadic · foci · membership closure

coral dashed = the new link formed.
📖 the three closures
  • Triadic closure — two people with a common friend befriend each other.
  • Foci closure — two people meet through a shared focus (club, class, gym) and befriend.
  • Membership closure — you bring an existing friend into a focus you belong to (they become a member because you are).

A focus (plural foci) is a shared activity/place that draws people together.

📈 quantifying closure pressure — the 1−(1−p)^k law

More common friends → more pressure to befriend. If a single common friend gives probability p of connecting, then k independent common friends all failing has probability $(1-p)^k$, so the chance they do connect is:

$$ T(k) = 1 - (1-p)^{k} $$

On real e-mail data this matches the (roughly linear, small) observed rise. We'll reuse this exact formula for all three closures in code.

✍️ worked example — closure probability

p = 0.1, k = 3 common neighbours: $1-(1-0.1)^3 = 1-0.9^3 = 1-0.729 = \boxed{0.271}$.

LECTURES 46–53

The Fatman Evolutionary Model

a city that evolves under homophily, closure & social influence — in NetworkX

5 Simulating a city

We build a "city": 100 person nodes (each with a random BMI) + 5 focus nodes (gym, eat-out, movie, karate, yoga). Every node carries two attributes: name (the BMI for a person, or the label for a focus) and type ('person' or 'foci'). Each person starts linked to exactly one random focus; foci never link to each other.

⚙️ build order (memorise)
  1. Create the graph + add 100 person nodes.
  2. Assign each a random BMI (name) and type='person'.
  3. Add the 5 focus nodes.
  4. Link each person to exactly one random focus.
  5. Each round: apply homophily → closure → change BMIs (social influence), and save a snapshot.
fatman_build.pypython
import networkx as nx, random, math

def create_graph():
    G = nx.Graph()
    G.add_nodes_from(range(1, 101))            # 100 person nodes: 1..100
    return G

def assign_bmi(G):
    for v in G.nodes():
        G.node[v]['name'] = random.randint(15, 40)   # BMI 15..40 (stored in 'name')
        G.node[v]['type'] = 'person'

def add_foci_nodes(G):
    i = G.number_of_nodes() + 1                # start numbering after the people
    foci = ['gym', 'eat out', 'movie club', 'karate club', 'yoga club']
    for j in range(0, 5):
        G.add_node(i)
        G.node[i]['name'] = foci[j]            # the focus label (stored in 'name')
        G.node[i]['type'] = 'foci'
        i = i + 1

def add_foci_edges(G):
    foci = [v for v in G.nodes() if G.node[v]['type'] == 'foci']
    persons = [v for v in G.nodes() if G.node[v]['type'] == 'person']
    for person in persons:
        r = random.choice(foci)                # one random focus per person
        G.add_edge(person, r)
⚠️ why one attribute called name for both?

A person's key feature is its BMI; a focus's is its label. Storing both under name lets one function (labels, sizes, colours) work for either kind; type tells them apart. (NetworkX 1.x uses G.node[v]; in 2.x it's G.nodes[v].)

Homophily — closer BMIs, higher link probability

homophily link probability
$$ p = \frac{1}{\,|\,\text{BMI}_u-\text{BMI}_v\,| + 1000\,} $$
read it as: "the closer their BMIs, the higher the chance they befriend." The +1000 does two jobs: (1) avoids divide-by-zero when BMIs are equal (gap 0), and (2) scales all probabilities down so edges accrue gradually.
fatman_homophily.pypython
def homophily(G):
    pnodes = [v for v in G.nodes() if G.node[v]['type'] == 'person']
    for u in pnodes:
        for v in pnodes:
            if u != v:
                gap = abs(G.node[u]['name'] - G.node[v]['name'])   # BMI difference
                p = 1.0 / (gap + 1000)          # closer BMI -> higher p
                if random.random() < p:         # realise an event of probability p
                    G.add_edge(u, v)
✍️ worked example — p for BMIs 22 & 27

gap = |22 − 27| = 5, so $p = \frac{1}{5+1000} = \frac{1}{1005}\approx \boxed{0.000995}$.

📖 the "draw and compare" trick — if random.random() < p

random.random() returns a uniform real in [0,1]. The chance it lands in [0, p) is exactly p, so if random.random() < p: do X makes X happen with probability p — the standard way to simulate any probabilistic event.

Closure — the 1−(1−p)^k law, applied to shared neighbours

fatman_closure.pypython
def cmn(u, v, G):                              # count common neighbours
    nu = set(G.neighbors(u))
    nv = set(G.neighbors(v))
    return len(nu & nv)                         # set intersection size

def closure(G):
    p = 0.01                                   # one common neighbour's contribution
    array1 = []                                # decide ALL edges first (independence)
    for u in G.nodes():
        for v in G.nodes():
            # at least one must be a person (two foci never share/close an edge)
            if u != v and (G.node[u]['type'] == 'person' or G.node[v]['type'] == 'person'):
                k = cmn(u, v, G)               # common neighbours
                prob = 1 - math.pow(1 - p, k)  # 1-(1-p)^k
                array1.append([u, v, prob])
    for u, v, prob in array1:                  # now realise them
        if random.uniform(0, 1) < prob:
            G.add_edge(u, v)
⚙️ one formula, all three closures
  • k = len(set(G.neighbors(u)) & set(G.neighbors(v))) = common neighbours. A shared neighbour can be a person (→ triadic closure) or a focus (→ foci/membership closure) — the same $1-(1-p)^k$ handles all three.
  • The guard type=='person' or ... requires at least one person: two foci are never linked, so an edge between two foci is meaningless.
  • Edges are collected into array1 first, then added — so each edge's decision is independent of edges added earlier in the same round.

Social influence — implemented indirectly via the foci

fatman_influence.pypython
def change_bmi(G):
    foci = [v for v in G.nodes() if G.node[v]['type'] == 'foci']
    for f in foci:
        if G.node[f]['name'] == 'eat out':
            for nb in G.neighbors(f):
                if G.node[nb]['name'] != 40:            # cap at 40
                    G.node[nb]['name'] = G.node[nb]['name'] + 1   # gain weight
        if G.node[f]['name'] == 'gym':
            for nb in G.neighbors(f):
                if G.node[nb]['name'] != 15:            # floor at 15
                    G.node[nb]['name'] = G.node[nb]['name'] - 1   # lose weight
read it as: "each round, gym members lose 1 BMI (never below 15); eat-out members gain 1 BMI (never above 40)." Social influence emerges indirectly: membership closure pulls your friends into your gym, and then they start losing weight too — your habit spread to them without any direct "make obese people friends" rule.

The main loop + storing longitudinal data

fatman_run.pypython
G = create_graph()
assign_bmi(G)
add_foci_nodes(G)
add_foci_edges(G)
nx.write_gml(G, 'evolution_0.gml')             # snapshot BEFORE evolving

for t in range(0, 10):                         # 10 rounds
    homophily(G)                               # 1) similar BMIs befriend
    closure(G)                                 # 2) shared neighbours befriend
    change_bmi(G)                              # 3) social influence via gym/eat-out
    nx.write_gml(G, 'evolution_' + str(t + 1) + '.gml')   # save each round

# later analysis: for each snapshot, H = nx.read_gml('evolution_i.gml');
# density RISES steadily as homophily + closures keep adding edges.
# obesity count = number of person nodes whose 'name' (BMI) == 40.
⚠️ Fatman model — key facts & gotchas
  • Save with nx.write_gml('evolution_t.gml'), reload with nx.read_gml. Plotting density across rounds → it rises (edges keep being added).
  • Obesity = count of person nodes with BMI (name) == 40; underweight = BMI == 15.
  • Social influence is not "add edges between obese people" — it's the gym/eat-out BMI change plus membership closure.
  • Assumption: only new edges appear (existing ones aren't deleted). Easy to extend.

the Fatman city (persons blue/green/yellow, foci red)

green = losing weight (gym), yellow = obese (eat-out), blue = normal, red = foci. Dashed = a homophily edge across.

Over the rounds you watch homophily wire similar-BMI people together, closures thicken the clusters, and the gym/eat-out foci pull members' BMIs apart — so obesity visibly spreads through the friendship structure, exactly the contagion we started with.

Similar people befriend (selection/homophily) and friends grow similar (influence) — and you can simulate the whole thing.

homophily H = 1 − actual/expected cross-group edges (H>0 homophily, 0 random, <0 heterophily) · similarity = shared/union · closure prob = 1−(1−p)^k · Fatman link p = 1/(|ΔBMI|+1000) with if random.random() < p.

  1. "Birds of a feather flock together" names which phenomenon?
    Homophily — similar people tend to befriend one another.
  2. Classify: two French-speakers befriend / you smoke after befriending a smoker / strangers meet at yoga / you bring a friend to your music club.
    Selection / social influence / foci closure / membership closure, respectively.
  3. Similarity of Anita {chess,hiking,jazz} and Bina {hiking,jazz,baking,cycling}?
    shared/union = {hiking,jazz}/{chess,hiking,jazz,baking,cycling} = 2/5 = 0.4.
  4. Club of 20 (10+10), 30 friendships, 9 cross-year — homophily value?
    Expected cross = ½·30 = 15; H = 1 − 9/15 = 0.4 (positive → homophily).
  5. What do H > 0, H = 0, H < 0 mean?
    Positive = homophily; 0 = chance-level (random) mixing; negative = heterophily (more cross-group ties than chance).
  6. In the Wikipedia study, what separates selection from influence?
    Similarity rise before first contact = selection; rise after = influence. On Wikipedia the pre-contact (selection) rise was steeper.
  7. Contagion findings (Christakis–Fowler)?
    Both obesity and happiness are contagious; happiness spreads up to three levels out.
  8. How is a person node initialised in the Fatman model?
    name = random.randint(15,40) (its BMI) and type = 'person'.
  9. Homophily p = 1/(|ΔBMI|+1000) for BMIs 22 & 27 — value and why +1000?
    $p = 1/1005 ≈ 0.000995$. +1000 avoids divide-by-zero (equal BMIs) and scales all probabilities down (gradual growth).
  10. Closure prob with p = 0.1 and k = 3 common neighbours?
    $1-(1-0.1)^3 = 1-0.729 = 0.271$.
  11. Why must at least one of the two closure nodes be a person?
    Foci are never linked to each other, so an edge between two foci is meaningless — closure applies to person–person or person–foci pairs.
  12. How is social influence implemented, and how does density change over rounds?
    Gym neighbours lose 1 BMI (floor 15), eat-out neighbours gain 1 BMI (cap 40); membership closure spreads it. Saved via nx.write_gml; density rises over rounds.
